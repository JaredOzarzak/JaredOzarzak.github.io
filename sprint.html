Sprinting with a Prosthesis: What Changes?

Why I did this

The question

How does a running prosthesis change the way someone sprints? We compared a sprinter with a below-knee prosthesis to a sprinter without one, looking at force, muscle activity, and joint angles while the foot is on the ground. This was a pilot study for my grad biomechanics class, run in the PLNU × San Diego Padres Biomechanics Lab.

The data

What we collected

  • 10 sprint trials: 5 from the runner with the prosthesis, 5 from the control runner
  • 3 data sources synced together: force plates (AMTI), muscle activity/EMG (Delsys), and markerless motion capture (Theia3D)
  • Left leg = intact leg, right leg = prosthesis side
How I did it

Step by step

1. Clean and line it up (Python)

Loaded the exported files in pandas and stretched every ground contact to the same 101 points, so trials of different lengths could be compared apples to apples.

2. Compare the whole curve, not just one number

Used SPM (statistical parametric mapping), which runs a t-test across the entire ground contact. That shows when the two runners differ, not just whether they do.

3. Compare peak joint angles

Ran t-tests and boxplots on peak hip, knee, and ankle angles.

What I found

The results

Intact leg

  • Bent less at the hip and knee than the control (p < 0.001 and p = 0.027), a stiffer way of running
  • Pushed more force into the ground in the middle of contact
  • Hamstrings worked harder early; calves and quads worked harder late

Prosthesis side

  • Peak force came later, in the last 20% of contact, from the spring-like blade
  • Knee bent more than the control (p = 0.002)

Big picture: the intact leg did most of the stabilizing and pushing, while the prosthesis side relied on energy coming back from the blade.

The catch

Limitations

It's one person per group with 5 trials each, so these results describe these two runners. They shouldn't be generalized to every sprinter with a prosthesis.

Short version

What I'd tell you in 30 seconds

I combined 3 types of data into one clean dataset, then compared two runners across the whole ground contact instead of just one number. The intact leg carried most of the load. With only 2 runners, it's a pilot study, not a final answer.
